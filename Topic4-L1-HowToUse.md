# Topic 4, Lecture 1 — How to Use the AI Tutor Pack

You're learning Lecture 1 on your own before class, with an AI doing the teaching. Download
**`Topic4-L1-AI-Tutor.md`**, hand it to an AI, and it runs a ~60–75 minute session with you.

## Start

1. Open **Claude, ChatGPT, or Gemini** — whichever you have. Use the **best model** you have
   access to, not the fastest one.
2. **Upload `Topic4-L1-AI-Tutor.md` — and only that file.** No upload button? Paste the whole
   file into the chat. Keep the Lecture 1 slides **open beside you**, but don't upload them
   (see below).
3. Send exactly this:

> I've uploaded my operating systems study pack. Read the INSTRUCTIONS FOR THE AI ASSISTANT
> section and follow it exactly. Start me at Stage 0.

That's all. It takes over from there.

## It asks you things. You ask it things. Both directions matter.

Most of the session is the AI asking you questions and making you work out the answer. But every
stage also stops and hands **you** the floor: *"What about this doesn't sit right? Ask me."*

Take that seriously. Working out **what to ask** is the harder half of understanding something,
and it's the part that keeps paying off long after this exam — it's most of what separates people
who get value out of an AI from people who get fluent-sounding noise.

Two things follow from that:

- **"I don't have any questions" usually means "I haven't poked at it yet."** The AI is instructed
  not to accept it the first time — it'll offer you a couple of things people normally find odd
  about that topic. Pick one.
- **A vague question will get sharpened, not answered.** Ask "I don't get DMA" and it'll come back
  with "what part — who moves the bytes, or why that's faster?" That's not it being difficult.
  Narrowing the question is the skill.

Everything you *can't* get settled goes on entry ticket (d), and that's what we open class with.

## Slides open beside you — but don't read ahead

This is a conversation, not a reading assignment. **The AI teaches you each idea**; you work it
out by talking to it. The slides are where you go *afterwards* to check whether what you worked
out matches what the course actually says.

That order matters. Working something out and then checking it sticks far better than reading it
and then being quizzed — and it means that when the slide and your version differ, you notice,
which is the whole point.

So: chat on one side, slides on the other, and don't skip ahead to the slides for a stage you
haven't worked through yet. You'd only be stealing the answer from yourself.

**Don't upload the deck.** The tutor pack already has every number and definition the AI needs,
and piling 37 more slides on top makes it likelier to drift into lecturing at you.

Two slides are worth looking at carefully when the AI sends you there: **System Stack** in Stage
1, and **File Descriptor & Open File Table** in Stage 2 — that second one is the diagram you have
to redraw for entry ticket (c), so see the real one. Parts 4 and 5 of the deck have no figures at
all, so you'll be working from the conversation there.

Stuck on one specific diagram? Screenshot that single slide and paste it in. That's fine.

## Three things to know before you start

**Start two days before class, not the night before.** Stage 0 is a 10-minute check on material
from Topic 1. If it tells you to go back and review, you need the time to actually do that.
Don't skip Stage 0 because you "already covered" that — it's the whole reason the later stages
make sense.

**Expect 60–80 back-and-forth messages.** This is a conversation, not a question. If your AI has
a message cap, you may hit it partway through, so **plan on two sittings** — Stages 0–3, then
4–6. At every stage the AI gives you a one-line progress note. Save it. Paste it with the file
into a new chat and it picks up where you left off.

**Use the strongest model you can.** The pack works by having the AI *attack* your explanations.
Weaker and faster models are much more agreeable — they'll nod along at a vague answer, and
you'll finish the hour feeling fine and knowing nothing.

## When the AI drifts — and it will

Over a long session, AIs slide back into lecturing. Recognize it and correct it:

| It's doing this | Paste this |
|---|---|
| Writing walls of text | "One question at a time. Stop and wait for my answer." |
| Giving the answer before you tried | "Don't tell me yet — let me attempt it first." |
| Saying "great start" to something you doubt | "Was that actually correct? Be strict with me." |
| Lecturing again after a few stages | "Go back to the rules in the instructions section and follow them." |

Expect to need the last one around Stage 3 or 4.

## If you only have 30 minutes

Don't just start at Stage 1 and run out. Do **Stage 0 → Stage 4 → Stage 2**, skip the rest.
That covers both of the hardest ideas and gives you everything the entry ticket needs. Not as
good as the full hour, much better than a half-finished run.

## Two rules

**Don't tell it you understand when you don't.** It can't tell the difference, it will believe
you, and it will move on. The only person that fools is you.

**If the AI and the slides disagree, the slides win** — and write the disagreement down. That's
what part (d) of the entry ticket is for. Don't quietly assume you misread the slide.

## What to bring to class

The **entry ticket** the AI gives you at the end. One page, handwritten is fine. The AI is
instructed not to solve it for you, and it won't — doing it yourself is the point.

Part (d) asks for two questions you couldn't settle. That's required, and it isn't an admission
that you failed: it's what we start class with.
